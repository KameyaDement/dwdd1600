The 5 Major Ecological Zones

Main Zones

Hawaii is divided into five major ecological zones determined by elevation, rainfall, and wind patterns, compressing nearly every climate on Earth into a single archipelago.

Coastal & Intertidal Zone
This zone encompasses everything from sandy beaches and rocky tidepools to brackish anchialine pools. Plants and animals here must be highly salt-, sun-, and wind-tolerant. You'll find native shoreplants like naupaka, alongside protected marine sanctuaries that host green sea turtles (honu) and vibrant coral reefs.
Tropical Rainforest
Located mostly on the windward (eastern) slopes of the islands, these areas receive massive amounts of trade-wind rainfall. These lush jungles feature dense canopy layers dominated by native ʻōhiʻa and koa trees, giant tree ferns, and mosses. They serve as vital watersheds that filter and recharge the islands' freshwater aquifers.
Mesic Forest
Mesic forests receive a moderate, balanced amount of rainfall and act as the transition zone between wet and dry environments. Because conditions here are less harsh than the extremes, this zone naturally supports the highest diversity of native plants and birds in the islands, making it a critical focus for local conservation efforts.
Tropical Dry Forest
Found on the leeward (western) sides of the islands, these areas sit in the rain shadow of giant volcanoes and receive very little rain. It is one of the most endangered ecosystems in the world. Unique, drought-resistant endemic flora like the ancient wiliwili tree and the rare kauila thrive in these rugged, sun-baked lava landscapes.
Subalpine & Alpine Desert
Exclusive to high-elevation summits like Mauna Kea, Mauna Loa, and Haleakalā, this zone sits above the cloud layer (typically starting around 6,000 to 9,000 feet). The climate is arid, wind-swept, and subject to freezing temperatures and winter snow. This harsh, lunar-like landscape is home to highly specialized species, including the iconic, silver-haired Haleakalā silversword (ʻāhinahina).

Sub Ecosystems

From snow-capped alpine peaks to sunlit coral reefs, Hawaii contains an astonishing concentration of the world's ecosystems, compressing nearly all of Earth's climate zones into a single isolated archipelago.

Terrestrial & Upland Ecosystems

  • Alpine Deserts
  • Subalpine Grasslands and Shrublands
  • Tropical Rainforests (Montane & Lowland Moist)
  • Tropical Dry Forests
  • Mesic Forests
  • Bogs and Montane Wetlands
  • Pioneer Volcanic Lava Flows

Aquatic & Subterranean Ecosystems

  • Anchialine Pools
  • Subterranean Lava Tubes
  • Perennial Streams and Riparian Zones

Coastal & Marine Ecosystems

  • Coastal Sand Dunes and Strands
  • Rocky Intertidal Zones and Tidepools
  • Estuaries and Mudflats
  • Shallow Coral Reefs
  • Mesophotic (Deep) Coral Reefs
  • Pelagic (Open Ocean) Zones

The striking diversity of these interconnected ecological zones and sub-ecosystems highlights Hawaii's status as a global crucible of evolution and ecological rarity. From the dark depths of subterranean lava tubes to the freezing expanses of alpine summits, each distinct habitat has fueled the spectacular adaptation of unique species found nowhere else on earth. Safeguarding these fragile systems is critical, as they not only protect Hawaii's irreplaceable biodiversity but also sustain the vital natural resources that support life across the islands.
